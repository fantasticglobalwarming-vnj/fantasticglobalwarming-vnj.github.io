Module 1 – What Are Greenhouse Gases?

Greenhouse gases are gases present in Earth’s atmosphere that have the ability to absorb and re-emit infrared radiation. This process traps heat close to Earth’s surface and is known as the greenhouse effect. Naturally occurring greenhouse gases such as water vapor, carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and nitrous oxide (N₂O) are essential for maintaining Earth’s average temperature. Without them, the planet would be too cold to support most life. However, human activities like burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial processes have significantly increased greenhouse gas concentrations. This enhanced greenhouse effect leads to global warming, which drives climate change, sea level rise, and extreme weather events.

Module 2 – Why They Matter

Greenhouse gases matter because they control how much heat stays on Earth. When sunlight reaches the planet, some energy is reflected back into space, but greenhouse gases absorb and trap part of this heat, keeping Earth warm enough for life. Human activities have increased greenhouse gas concentrations far beyond natural levels. This causes the planet to warm faster than ecosystems can adapt. The result includes melting glaciers, rising sea levels, stronger storms, and more frequent heatwaves. Understanding why greenhouse gases matter helps explain why reducing emissions is essential to limit global warming and protect human societies.

Module 3 – Major Contributors

The largest contributors to greenhouse gas emissions come from human economic activities. The burning of coal, oil, and natural gas for electricity, transportation, and industry is the biggest source of carbon dioxide (CO₂). Agriculture is another major contributor, especially through livestock, which release methane (CH₄) during digestion. The use of fertilizers produces nitrous oxide (N₂O), a gas with a very strong warming effect. Waste management, deforestation, and industrial chemicals also add to emissions, making climate change a problem driven mainly by human choices.

Module 4 – Measuring GHGs

Greenhouse gases are measured using scientific instruments on the ground, in aircraft, and by satellites. Concentrations of gases like CO₂ are commonly expressed in parts per million (ppm), which shows how many gas molecules exist per million air molecules. Long-term measurements allow scientists to track trends and identify human influence on the atmosphere. Ice cores, for example, provide records of greenhouse gas levels going back hundreds of thousands of years. Accurate measurement is critical for climate models, policies, and international agreements aimed at reducing emissions.

Module 5 – Emission Sources

Emissions come from many everyday activities. Transportation releases CO₂ through cars, planes, and ships that burn fossil fuels. Electricity production often relies on coal and natural gas, making it one of the largest emission sources. Food systems also play a role, including farming, processing, packaging, and transportation. Even household energy use contributes to emissions through heating, cooling, and electricity consumption. This shows that climate change is closely connected to daily human behavior.

Module 6 – Gas Strength

Not all greenhouse gases warm the planet equally. Scientists compare their strength using Global Warming Potential (GWP), which measures how much heat a gas traps over a certain time period compared to carbon dioxide. Methane (CH₄) is far more powerful than CO₂ in the short term, even though it stays in the atmosphere for a shorter time. Nitrous oxide (N₂O) is even stronger and lasts much longer. Understanding gas strength helps prioritize which emissions should be reduced first to slow warming effectively.

Module 7 – Feedback Loops & Tipping Points

Feedback loops occur when climate changes trigger effects that further increase warming. For example, melting ice reduces Earth’s reflectivity, causing more sunlight to be absorbed and leading to even more warming. Some systems may reach tipping points, where changes become irreversible. Once crossed, returning to the original climate state becomes extremely difficult. These processes make climate change faster and more dangerous than simple warming alone.

Module 8 – Carbon Budgets & Pathways

A carbon budget represents the maximum amount of CO₂ humanity can emit while still limiting global warming to a specific level, such as 1.5°C or 2°C. Scientists calculate these budgets based on climate models and remaining atmospheric capacity. Exceeding the budget increases the risk of severe climate impacts. Emission pathways describe how quickly emissions must decline to stay within these limits.

Module 10 – Technology: Removal & Mitigation

Technology plays a major role in addressing climate change. Renewable energy sources such as solar and wind produce electricity without direct greenhouse gas emissions. Other technologies focus on carbon capture and storage (CCS), which removes CO₂ from industrial processes or the atmosphere. Energy efficiency also reduces emissions by using less energy overall. Technological solutions are essential but must be combined with behavioral and policy changes.

Module 11 – Residual Emissions

Some emissions are extremely difficult to eliminate, such as those from aviation or certain industrial processes. These are known as residual emissions. To balance them, societies may need negative emissions, where more CO₂ is removed from the atmosphere than released. Examples include reforestation and direct air capture. Managing residual emissions is crucial for reaching net-zero goals.
